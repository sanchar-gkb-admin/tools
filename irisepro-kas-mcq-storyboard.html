The n-gate on question 19 matters more than it looks. The equivalence rule was p_max − p_min ≤ 0.10 across a slot's questions. That is a range statistic, and a threshold on a range means nothing without n. The expected range of 22 perfectly equivalent questions under the null is d₂(22)·√(0.25/n) = 0.349 at n=30 · 0.270 at n=50 · 0.191 at n=100. So as written the rule quarantines healthy slots. The author pulls a good question, the pool drops below target_n, generation degrades, and nothing says so. No verdict may render before it can be true.

Waiting for n is necessary and not sufficient. Solving that expression for 0.10 gives n ≈ 365 responses per question — 8,030 per slot, 803,000 per paper, about 380 Advanced students per paper before the check that the design calls "the only real equivalence check" can speak at all. But choosing n so that the expected null range equals the threshold means, by definition, half of perfectly equivalent slots still exceed it. So the fix is not "wait longer" — it is a proper test: a two-proportion z of each question against its slot's pooled p excluding itself, with the family-wise correction stated (Bonferroni zcrit = 3.05 across one slot's 22 questions, 4.24 across a paper's 2,200). A02 must also say which checks are live and which are dormant, so nobody reads a quiet slot as a validated one.
Same cause, different symptom. A question added in month 6 is only ever drawn by students already ten attempts in — a more practised, self-selected population. Its p is inflated by cohort, not difficulty. So the rule's most reliable behaviour is to flag the newest content as too easy, which is exactly the content the 137-of-400 shortfall depends on. Store p_value_by_attempt_index[], not a scalar, and compare questions only at the same attempt index.

The tier ladder is gone, and it took a problem with it. target_n was a single scalar while the old tiers needed 4 / 8 / 22 — one slot cannot hold three values, and sizing separately meant Basic and Advanced students answered different item sets with incomparable percentages. Under the set model there is one number: 9 questions — 8 sets plus a spare. Every student draws from one pool. 1,800 questions across both papers instead of 4,400, a 59% cut, and one author can finish it in 3.6–6 months.

And the weights are not ours to write. KMM authors the ability mapping; this screen validates it. That is the right split — the judgement about what a question demands belongs to the framework's author, and the arithmetic about whether the resulting vector can support a claim belongs here.

And position must be randomised per student. Slot identity was fixed to (paper_id, seq_no) with shuffling only within an area — but both source papers are positionally blocked by area (GS-1's 16 areas fall in 22 contiguous runs; GS-2 is three blocks of 40/30/30). So position predicts area and area predicts ability, for every student, on every attempt, forever. By attempt 5 a student has the blueprint; one study group has the paper's full specification. Randomising position costs nothing now and is a migration later.
Two numbers, and this row previously conflated them. It read “Position in paper — randomised per student, never a fixed seq_no”. seq_no is precisely fixed: it is the slot’s identity, unique within the paper, the order the draw walks, and half the primary key of every response row. What is randomised is presentation_order, a different number living on the student’s paper.

Write the screen position into mcq_responses.seq_no and every answer is attributed to the wrong slot — therefore to the wrong weight vector, therefore to the wrong abilities. It would not throw, it would not look wrong, and the scorecard would be confidently, invisibly false. The caption was an instruction to build exactly that.

And the shuffle is bounded by area (shuffleWithinArea), so “randomised” was never the whole truth either: a History slot stays among History questions. A candidate who has budgeted their time by section still finds the paper in sections.
What each field on this screen is actually for — traced to its consumer, not assumed.

Area — two real consumers. It bounds the shuffle, and it is the “where did I lose marks” breakdown on the scorecard. The only field here the running system reads twice.

Difficulty band — one intended consumer, currently unwired. The archetype carries ideal_seconds {"easy":45,"medium":60,"hard":90}, keyed by exactly this enum — so the band is what sets a slot’s per-question time benchmark, and the 60s in the student’s pace bar comes from here. No spec line says that lookup happens. The link is implied by the enum values matching, which is not a link. It is also the one field with a way to check itself: observed proportion-correct against the declared band, once responses exist.

Topic and cognitive demand — read by people, not by code. Zero runtime consumers, and that is not a defect. Their job was to construct the blueprint — grouping the existing questions by topic × ability set × band is how the 100 slots came to exist — and now they tell an author what a replacement question has to be. Calling that out matters because a field with no consumer is normally a bug, and these two are the exception rather than an oversight.

Four affordances that are not decoration — each one is 1,800×.

Autosave, on the create path. The task editor already has one; a new MCQ question did not, so a dropped connection cost the twenty minutes since the author opened the page. A draft with no id is exactly when a loss is total.

Save & add another, and Duplicate. The nine questions of a slot are structurally identical — same area, same demand, same weights, same shape — and differ only in stem and options. Returning to the workbench between each one turns nine questions into nine navigations. Duplicate carries the shape and deliberately clears the stem, options and concept key, so it cannot become a copy-paste route to a near-duplicate.

error_type is a closed list, not free text. It was a required field with no defined values across 13,200 distractors. Free text there is unaggregatable by construction — “confuses cause and trigger” and “mistakes the trigger for the cause” are the same diagnosis and will never group. As an enum it powers the student’s “you made this error 4 times” line; as prose it powers nothing.

The near-duplicate warning fires on the concept key, live. I4 promises a student never sees the same concept twice across 1,600 draws. That promise is kept by an index at draw time — but discovering the clash then means a paper that cannot be built. Catching it while the author is still on the page costs one line.

One question, N renderings — and the split is not cosmetic.

Per language: the stem, the four option texts, the explanation, and the misconception prose. Not per language: which option is correct, the concept_key, each distractor’s error_type, shuffle_safe, the slot’s weights, the timing. A diagnosis is a fact about the option, not about its wording — so a Kannada candidate picking C and an English candidate picking C made the same error, and the scorecard can say so.

Model each language as its own question row and three things break at once. unique(slot_id, question_no) stops holding. The draw counts 27 questions in a slot that has 9, so it believes it has three times the bank it has. And a student could draw the English and the Kannada rendering of one item across their eight sets — the exact repeat I4 exists to forbid, invisible to an index keyed on the question id.

An item goes live only when every required language has passed review. Per-language availability sounds kinder to the schedule and is the trap: it silently gives Kannada candidates a smaller pool per slot, and the no-repeat guarantee is arithmetic over that pool. Nine questions per slot has to mean nine in every language on offer, or it means nothing for the candidate sitting in Kannada.
The gates must re-run per language, and this item proves why with its own numbers. Rank held — the correct option is 3rd of 4 in both scripts. Spread did not. English runs 22–29 characters, a ratio of 1.32×; the same four options in Kannada run 12–26, a ratio of 2.17×, which clears the old 2.0× ceiling that the English rendering passes comfortably.

One item, one gate, two different verdicts — from translation alone. So a bank certified in English is not certified. Kannada is agglutinative and written in a script with its own density; character counts are simply a different measurement there, and any rule expressed in characters has to be evaluated where it is read.

(Rank being stable here is one item, not a finding — it is the reason the rank budget is tracked per language too rather than assumed to travel.)
The friction is deliberate — requiring a named misconception per wrong option is what prevents fillers like "only mountain climbing". It was assumed this fought the length rule, since real distractors carry qualifying clauses and fillers are two words. We ran the exemplar pair. It doesn't.

Take a genuine higher-order item whose correct option needs a clause (129 chars) against lazy distractors (28, 40, 18): spread 7.17×, fails. Now rewrite those three as beliefs candidates actually hold — the 42nd Amendment origin story, the State-vs-Union confusion, the Article 31C inversion — and they come out at 84, 82, 73. Spread 1.77×, passes. Writing better distractors closes the gap rather than widening it, because a real misconception needs about as much qualification as a real answer.

But the correct option is still the longest of the four. The item satisfies the rule and fails the rule's purpose — which is the actual defect: max/min ≤ 2.0× constrains spread, and the tell lives in rank. Trim the correct option to 57 chars and it becomes the shortest of the four, spread 1.47×, still a higher-order item with three real misconceptions, tell gone.
So the rule changes. Length-ratio is replaced by rank uniformity: across the bank — and per ability, since the tell concentrates in Comprehension and Critical & Analytical — the correct option should be the longest about a quarter of the time, the shortest about a quarter, and so on. χ²(3) against uniform, critical value 7.815. This subsumes the old rule and closes its mirror exploit: a bank at 0% longest-correct passes a one-sided ceiling while making "never pick the longest" worth roughly 22 marks.

No per-question verdict — a single item's rank carries no information. A04 shows a running budget instead, and the whole discipline is one sentence an author can hold: roughly one question in four should have the shortest option correct.

Four things this screen does that a per-question create page cannot.

It removes 3,400 page loads. Nine questions via a create-page-and-return costs 18 navigations per slot; across 200 slots that is 3,600. Filling in place costs one load per slot — 200. Nothing about the writing got easier, but 18× of the sitting-down-and-getting-back went away.

The six siblings are on screen while you write the seventh. The near-duplicate warning in A04 fires after you have written the question; the concept list in the rail prevents you writing it. Same inpaper, one prevents and one scolds.

It asks for the three misconceptions before the three options. The stem is not what takes fifteen minutes — three real beliefs a candidate holds is. Choosing them from a list that has learned this topic’s recurring errors turns the hardest step into a pick, and it is the same closed error_type enum that makes the student’s “you made this error 4 times” line possible. One decision paying twice.

It answers “what next” without a menu. 200 slots is not a choice anyone should make 200 times. The next-starved-slot button is the same least-served-first ordering the draw already uses.
And one correction to my own design, forced by arithmetic. I had the length-rank budget as a per-slot meter. Per slot it is unmanageable. The target is the correct option being longest about a quarter of the time — 2.25 of 9. An author who reaches question 8 at 5 of 8 cannot recover: finishing perfectly still lands at 5 of 9 = 56%, against a 25% target. By the time a slot-level meter shows a problem, the slot is already spent.

So the budget is tracked at bank level and spent as an instruction: the rail does not report a slot verdict, it says “keep this one short” before the question is written. Same measurement, opposite direction — a gate that fires after the fact would simply quarantine work that is already paid for.
Two scales, and they are easy to swap. 100 is slots — one per question in the real paper, numbered 1–100, so slot 22 and slot 47 are ordinary neighbours. 9 is questions inside one slot: the eight a monthly student will draw across their eight sets, plus one spare. Every count on this board now carries its unit, because the screen previously showed a bare “6 of 9” beside a slot number and invited exactly the wrong reading.

And the 9 is a formula, not a constant. targetN(slot) = 8k + stream.question_spare — 9 for the eight-set product, 17 if a stream sells sixteen, 25 for twenty-four. Spare is a per-stream setting — how much quarantine headroom a bank needs is a property of that stream’s content, not of the platform. And a slot’s own target_n is nullable: it means override, not storage, so raising the stream setting cannot leave stale nines behind in 200 rows. One resolver, every caller.

Growing is additive and safe; promising is not. Questions 10–17 are new rows in existing slots, and every prior draw is already recorded, so extending a pool cannot disturb a student mid-journey. Raising sets_allowed before those questions exist is the unsafe order — the product promises a set the bank cannot draw and SlotStarved aborts both papers. One more 8-pack is 1,600 questions across the two papers, roughly 3.3 author-months.

One spare tolerates one quarantine per slot, and no more. Eight of nine get drawn; pull one question and eight remain, exactly enough. Pull a second and the eighth set cannot be built for anyone who reaches that slot. A06 exists because questions do get pulled — and the number is now a row, changeable without a deploy. Raising it is forward-looking: it marks slots as needing more questions and blocks a sets_allowed increase, but no student mid-journey is disturbed, because the draw aborts on their eligible set being empty, never on the authoring target.

Translating through the question editor would repeat the mistake A08 just fixed. A04b is the right screen for fixing one rendering; it is the wrong screen for 9,000 of them, for the same reason a create-page is wrong for 1,800 questions. Here the unit is a line: English left and locked, Kannada right and editable, tab moves down, and a slot’s 45 lines sit on one screen.

Seeded, not blank. Kannada is typed through transliteration and a blank column is the expensive starting state. This screen did not exist, and its absence was the largest hole on the board. Every other admin surface here — batches, campaigns, evaluation — has a second actor. MCQ questions went draft → live on a single author’s click, which means the four automated gates were the entire quality bar. Those gates check structure: a misconception is present, the rank budget holds, the item is safe to shuffle. Not one of them can tell whether the answer is correct. Both existing KAS answer keys are already known-bad — GS-1’s is a perfect A,B,C,D cycle, GS-2’s is 61% “A” — so “the author checked it” is a bar this content has demonstrably failed before.

A translation needs a reviewer who reads that language — an English speaker approving Kannada is a rubber stamp with a name on it, and the queue is filtered by what a reviewer can read. The reviewer may not be the author (row 4, locked), and a translator may not approve their own translation. Enforce it in the transition, not the UI: a self-approval that the screen hides but the route allows is not a control.
