KAS MCQ — the authoring admin

Twelve screens. The author's unit of work is a slot: a group of different questions sharing one topic, one cognitive demand, one ability set and one difficulty band.

This document is authoring only. The student experience moved to its own storyboard after a four-lens red team rewrote it. The evidence behind screens 01 and 09 is in the question × ability distribution map, parsed from the source papers and reconciled against their own summary tables.
EXISTS — already in the codebase EXTENDS — existing thing, changed NEW — built for MCQ

Read this first

We are building a mock-test engine for KAS Prelims. A student buys a set, sits GS-1 and GS-2, and gets back not just a mark but a picture of how they think — which abilities the paper found strong and which it did not.

To do that honestly, no two students may sit the identical paper, and no student may meet the same fact twice across the eight sets they buy. So we do not write “one paper”. We write a bank, and each student’s paper is drawn from it.

KAS Prelims has 2 papers — GS-1 and GS-2. Together they are one set.
  Each paper has 100 questions. Question 22 is always the same kind of question — same subject, same difficulty, same skills. We call that position a slot.
    For each slot we write 9 different questions on different facts — 8 so a monthly student never repeats, plus 1 spare.
      Each question is then translated — English, Kannada, Hindi. Same question, different language.

That is 100 × 9 × 2 papers = 1,800 questions to write, and every one of them translated. The screens that follow are the tools for doing that without losing your place.

Words used on these screens

Everything else is ordinary English. If a screen uses a term that is not here, that is a bug in the screen.

SetOne sitting of both papers — GS-1 and GS-2 together. What a student buys.
SlotA position in the paper. Question 22 is always the same kind of question: same subject, same difficulty, same abilities.
QuestionOne actual question written for a slot. Nine per slot, each on a different fact.
VariantThe same question in another language. Not a different question.
ConceptThe fact a question tests. Two questions asking the same fact in different words share one concept, so a student is only ever given one of them.
DistractorA wrong option. Each one names the mistaken belief that makes it tempting.
WeightHow much a slot demands of one ability, out of 100. Every slot rates all 15.
DrawChoosing which of a slot’s 9 questions this particular student gets.
QuarantineTaking a question out of circulation because something is wrong with it.
GateAn automatic check that stops a question going live.

The order is not a preference, and the screens refuse to let you skip it. A question cannot be sent for review until KMM’s abilities for that slot have landed — until then there is nothing to check it against. A slot cannot be translated until its English has been approved, because correcting a draft that later changes means doing the work twice. And a question cannot go live until every language on offer has been approved by someone who reads it — releasing one language early would quietly leave Kannada candidates with fewer questions to draw from, and the promise that nobody meets the same fact twice is counted against that smaller pool.

Each of the three is refused by the system at the moment you try, not hidden behind a greyed-out button.

04 · Write the nine questions for a slot

Write the nine questions for one slot. This is where most of the work happens.

  1. Read the slot's subject, difficulty and time limit, top left.
  2. Look at the concepts already used — your new question must be about something else.
  3. Type the question.
  4. Type the four options and mark the correct one.
  5. For each wrong option, pick the mistake it represents and write one line on why someone would choose it.
  6. Press Save and write the next one. Repeat until all nine are done.
  7. Then press next slot and start again.
Nine questions is one sitting's work. You can stop and come back — everything saves as you type.

3 · Review, then translate — repeat per language

06 · Approve someone else’s question

Read someone else's question and decide whether it can go live.

  1. Pick a question from the list. Your own are greyed out — you cannot approve your own work.
  2. Read it as a candidate would: is the marked answer definitely right, and only that one?
  3. Approve it, or write a note saying what to change and send it back.

07 · Translate a slot into another language

Translate a whole slot into one language.

  1. Choose the language and the slot.
  2. English is on the left and cannot be changed.
  3. A rough machine draft is already filled in on the right — correct it rather than typing from nothing.
  4. Tick every line you are happy with. An unticked line counts for nothing.
  5. When all lines are ticked, send the slot for review.
A reader of that language must approve it. Until then the translation is not live.

08 · Fix one translated question

  1. Change any of the words — question, options, explanations.

  3. Save, or go back to the English.

4 · Keep the bank honest — forever

09 · Keep the list of common mistakes short

Keep the list of common mistakes short and useful.

  1. Read the suggestions authors have sent while writing.
  2. For each one, either merge it into a mistake already on the list, or accept it as new.
  3. Watch the usage counts — one used only a handful of times across the whole bank should be merged away.
  4. Edit the explanation once and every question using it is updated.

10 · Keep the list of facts free of duplicates

Keep the list of facts clean, so no student meets the same fact twice.

  1. Start with the duplicate candidates at the top — pairs that look like the same fact in different words.
  2. For each pair, decide: same fact, or genuinely different?
  3. If the same, merge them.
  4. Browse by subject and topic to see what the bank covers and where it is thin.
Merging cannot be undone, so read both questions before you decide.

11 · Check the finished bank is sound

Check the finished bank has no pattern a candidate could exploit.

  1. Look at how the correct answers are spread across A, B, C and D.

  3. Any red check means some questions need rewriting — the screen names them.

12 · Take a bad question out

Take a bad question out of circulation.

  1. Read why it was flagged and how many students have answered it.
  2. Decide whether to pull it. Once pulled, nobody new is given it.
  3. Then decide separately what happens to the students who already answered it.
That second decision is not made here and is not yet settled — see Q2 in the open list.
