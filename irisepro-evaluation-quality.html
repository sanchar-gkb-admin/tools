Evaluation Quality

Three surfaces: what a Yashodarshi sees about their own marking, what an admin sees about one student, and what an admin sees about one Yashodarshi — plus the tools that make quality measurable rather than assumed.

The finding that makes this urgent

A child’s score depends heavily on who marks them. Measured across 10,578 live evaluations:

A 23-point spread between the most and least generous marker. Two children doing identical work receive materially different scores — and neither they nor their parents can see why. Platform-wide, feedback ranges from 0.3% to 99.8% depending on who you draw.

⛔ There is no single “quality score”, and building one would be the mistake

Prem Sagar — the evaluator in the 7 September parent escalation — is the best discriminator of the four: he varies his scores across abilities more than anyone (2.49 distinct values per sample, straight-lines only 2.1% of the time) and he spends more time per task than the platform average. His weakness is feedback, not judgement. Divya is simultaneously the most generous and the most undifferentiated. Collapse those into one number and you would rank the careful marker below the lenient one. Show the dimensions; let a human read them.

Why show an evaluator their own comparison

Nothing has ever told Prem Sagar that 93% of his evaluations went out with no feedback. He is not careless — he spends longer per task than almost anyone. A number he can see beats a policy he was never shown. Show the cohort range beside his own figure and let him place himself; do not show a target, which invites gaming.

The flag column is the whole design

An admin cannot read 21 tasks hunting for a problem. The screen must surface the anomaly. On this student two flags point straight at it — expired unsubmitted on position 11, submitted 11 seconds after prior expiry on position 12. Yesterday’s escalation took a day. With this row visible it is a five-minute conversation.

04

The tools — what to build, in order

Ranked by what they catch against what they cost. The first three run on data we already hold.

1 · Anomaly flags build first

Derived at read time, no new storage. Each one is a query we have already written by hand during the escalation:

  • Expired unsubmitted — the single most complaint-generating outcome we produce
  • Submitted within N seconds of a prior task expiring — this is exactly what happened on 4 September, and it has occurred once platform-wide, so the alert volume is near zero
  • Scored with no feedback — currently 72% of all evaluations
  • Evaluated in under 30 seconds — the platform median sitting is 72s; under 30 warrants a look
  • Straight-lined — the same value across all sampled abilities

2 · Evaluator scorecard build second

Screen 03 above, run weekly. Never a single composite number — four dimensions side by side with the cohort range, because the careful marker and the lenient one are distinguished only by looking at more than one.

3 · Feedback floor build third

Feedback is optional today and 72% of evaluations go out without it. Either make it required to submit an evaluation, or make “no feedback” a deliberate, logged choice with a reason. The current state — silently optional — is what produced this complaint.

4 · Calibration — the only thing that actually measures marking quality

Everything above measures behaviour. None of it can tell you whether a score is right, because there is no second opinion to compare against. This is how examination boards solve it, and it is the real answer to your question:

a · Double-marking sample

Route 1 in 20 submissions to a second Yashodarshi, blind. Compare the two scores. The spread between markers on identical work is the only honest measure of consistency we can produce — and it converts “Divya marks 23 points higher” from a suspicion into a number.

b · Anchor submissions

Take ten real submissions, have the Chief Yashodarshi mark them definitively, then quietly insert them into evaluators’ queues every few weeks. Deviation from the anchor is per-evaluator drift, measured against a known answer rather than against each other.

c · Re-mark on dispute

When a parent queries a score, a second evaluator marks it blind before anyone looks at the original. The dispute then resolves on evidence, not on seniority — and every dispute silently becomes another calibration data point.

5 · Rubric anchoring content, not code

A 23-point spread usually means the rubric is under-specified rather than that markers are careless. For each ability, write what a 2, a 3 and a 4 actually look like, with a real example. This is the cheapest large improvement available and it needs no engineering — but it does need the Chief Yashodarshi’s time.

⚠️ A history boundary every one of these screens must state

score_snapshot is absent on 7,178 of 10,578 evaluations — 67.9%. It is not a live bug: the snapshot begins in May 2026 and every one of the last 1,029 evaluations has it. The missing rows run 23 Dec 2025 → 26 May 2026, before the field existed.

The consequence is real anyway. No percentage can be computed for any evaluation before ~May 2026, because the maximum it was marked against was never recorded. Ten evaluators — id 43, 44, 45, 47, 48, 37, 57, 60, 55, 28 — have 100% of their work in that period and cannot appear in any score comparison at all. The four in the table above are the currently-active cohort, which is why they are the ones with data. Every screen here must say “from May 2026” rather than quietly showing a shorter history than it appears to.
