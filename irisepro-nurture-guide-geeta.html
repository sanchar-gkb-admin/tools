How we talk to people who are interested in YASHHO

Geeta ji — this guide explains how our WhatsApp follow-up will work, and asks you to fill in the parts only you can decide. There is nothing technical in here. Where something needs a decision from you, it is marked YOU DECIDE.

What this is for

When someone fills our form, we want to follow up on WhatsApp — helpfully, at the right time, and in their language. The computer will do the sending. But the computer does not know what to say, when to say it, or when to stop. That is what we need from you. Once you write it down here, we build exactly that.

1 How warm is this person?

Not everyone who fills our form is equally interested. Some are ready to pay today. Some were just curious. We should not talk to both the same way.

So we will describe every person with one of five words. The computer will work out which word fits, and will follow the rules you write in section 4.

WordWhat it meansExample

WARMInterested. Replying to us, reading our messagesHe replied to our WhatsApp and asked two questions about the batches
COLDGave us their details but has gone quiet sinceShe filled the form last week and has not opened anything since
SLEEPINGWas interested once, but that was a long time agoHe was warm two months ago, then nothing
STOPPEDAsked us to stop, or blocked usShe replied "STOP" — or blocked our number

Two more, which are not really about warmth:

NEWJust arrived. We do not know anything about them yet
PAIDThey have paid. They leave the follow-up list entirely and become a customer

The most important one is STOPPED

If someone tells us to stop, or blocks us, we never message them again — not even once, not even a helpful message. This is not just good manners. On WhatsApp, when someone blocks a number, they block everything from that number. So if a parent blocks us because of one message they did not want, we can no longer send them their child's scorecard or their login code either. We would lose them completely. This is the single most expensive mistake we can make.

2 Where are they in the journey?

"How warm" is only half the picture. The other half is where they have got to. Someone can be very warm but has not yet seen a demo — and someone can be lukewarm but has already sat through one. They need completely different messages.

So every person also has a stage. There are five, and they move in one direction.

StageWhere they have got toOur ONE goal at this stage
1 · Just arrivedThey gave us their details. They have not seen anything yetGet them to book a demo session
2 · Demo bookedThey have a seat in a sessionGet them to actually turn up
3 · Demo attendedThey have seen how it works and heard our success storiesHave the joining conversation
4 · Talking about joiningWe are discussing which programme and what it costsHelp them join at the level that is right for them
5 · JoinedThey have paidWelcome them properly. They leave this list

The rule that shapes everything

Nobody should be asked to join before they have seen a demo.

Unless they are already with us, of course. Our job with a brand new lead is not to sell — it is to get them into a session where they can see the platform working and hear from families like theirs. The demo does the selling. Everything we send before the demo has one purpose: get them into that room.

Booking a demo and not turning up is its own problem

This is the single biggest leak in most education businesses, and it needs its own treatment. Somebody who books and does not attend is not cold — they were interested enough to book. They got busy, or forgot, or something came up.

So a no-show needs a rescue, not a nurture. Offer the next session immediately and make it easy. Do not treat them like a cold lead — that loses someone who was nearly there.

The two things together

Stage tells us what to talk about. Temperature tells us how often and how urgently. A hot person at stage 1 gets pushed hard towards a demo. A cold person at stage 3 gets a gentle reminder of what they saw.

3 How we work out how warm someone is

We give points. Things that show interest add points. Silence takes points away. The total tells us which of the five words fits.

Think of it like this: if you saw only this one action, would you think this person is more likely to join, or less? And how much more? That is the points value.

Two questions only you can answer

  1. How many unanswered messages before we stop? If we keep messaging someone who is ignoring us, sooner or later they block us — and then we lose them for everything, forever. But if we stop too early we lose people who were simply busy. What feels right to you?
  2. What wakes someone up again? If a SLEEPING person suddenly opens our message — is that enough to treat them as WARM again, or should they have to actually reply first?

6 What we offer, and in what order

After the demo comes the joining conversation. We always start at the top.

Start with the best thing we have

Always open with the ₹4,999 full programme plus one year of mentoring.

Not because we want the biggest sale, but because it is the thing that actually works best for the child — 21 tasks instead of 7, the full ability picture, and a year of guidance rather than a few weeks. If we open with something smaller, we have decided on their behalf that they cannot afford it. That is their decision to make, not ours.

If they hesitate, come down one step at a time

People say no for different reasons, and each reason has a different next step. The order matters — please do not skip down the ladder.

StepWhat we offerUse this when they say…
1 · Start here₹4,999 full programme + 1 year mentoring
21 tasks · the complete ability picture		— (this is always the opening)
2₹4,999 programme on its own"The mentoring sounds like a lot to take on"
3₹4,999, paid in instalments"I want it, but not all at once right now"
4₹1,999 · 7 tasks"We do not have time for 21 tasks"
5 · Last step₹999 · 7 tasks, shorter"I want to see it work for my child before I spend more"

Why step 3 comes before step 4 — this matters

When money is the problem, the answer is instalments, not a smaller programme.

Dropping from ₹4,999 to ₹999 does two bad things at once: we earn ₹4,000 less, and the child gets a smaller, weaker first experience — which makes them less likely to continue with us. Splitting the payment keeps the full programme and solves the actual problem they raised. Only move to a smaller programme when the objection is genuinely about time or commitment, not money.

And please — do not offer the ladder before they ask for it

If we lead with "we also have a cheaper one", we have made the decision for them and lost the sale we could have had. Each step down should be a response to something they actually said, not something we volunteered. Sit with the pause after the price. Most people fill it themselves.

Using our families' stories

The demo does the convincing, but the stories are what people remember afterwards. In the joining conversation, use the story that matches the person in front of you — a KAS aspirant wants to hear about a KAS aspirant; a mother of a nine-year-old wants to hear about another mother of a nine-year-old.

One rule about children in our stories

We may share a family's story with their clear permission. But we must never use a child's actual marks or results to decide who we approach or what we offer them — see section 9, rule 1. A story is something a family chose to tell. A child's score is not.

7 The ten messages we need you to write

Two sets of five. We honestly do not know which one will work — so we are going to try five different reasons, send them in different orders to different people, and let the results tell us. That is why there are five and not one.

Set one comes BEFORE the demo. Its only job is to get them into the session.

Not to sell. Before someone has seen a demo they are not thinking about money at all — they are thinking "is this going to be a sales trap?", "how long will it take?", "do I need to prepare something?" So these five messages answer those worries, not the price question.

The thing we may not be saying loudly enough

The one year of mentoring is our strongest argument, and it is easy to mention it as an extra. ₹4,999 for 21 activities is a product. ₹4,999 for 21 activities and a mentor for a whole year is something else entirely — and it is the only reason the "₹14 a day" sum works. Please lead with the mentoring, not with the tasks.

⚠ One important warning about message 3

There is a version of "time is running out" that we must not write. Telling a parent their child's future is slipping away is frightening them, and we decided long ago that we do not sell with fear.

But there is a true version that is just as strong: abilities build up over time, so a year that starts now really is different from a year that starts in six months. That is simply a fact about how it works — not a warning about what will go wrong.

The test: read it back and ask — does this make a parent feel calm and clear, or anxious? If anxious, rewrite it. One is a calculation. The other is a threat.

Message 5 — the promise we make, and the one we ask for

This is the most important message of the ten, so here is the thinking behind it in full.

We do not give refunds. What we give instead is better.

A Yashodarshi who knows your child by name, for a whole year. A refund says "if we fail, you get your money back". A real person says "we will not let you fail on your own." For a parent choosing on behalf of their child, the second one means far more.

It is an agreement — a promise both ways

Not rules we impose. Two promises, made to each other.

What we promiseWhat we ask them to promise
A Yashodarshi who knows your child by name, for a full yearDo the tasks the way they are meant to be done
Every task read. Every question answeredFollow your Yashodarshi's guidance
An honest word the moment we see them driftingStay the course

Why this matters more than it looks: when someone gives their word out loud, they are far more likely to keep it. So asking for this promise at the start does not just protect us — it genuinely helps the child finish.

And they can see whether it is being kept

All three things we ask are things a Yashodarshi can see — tasks done or not, advice followed or not, still going or drifting. So we must notice early and say something kindly, in week three, not explain ourselves in month twelve.

If we only ever mention the agreement when something has gone wrong, it becomes an excuse. If the Yashodarshi is watching it from day one, it becomes the service.

⚠ One sentence we must never write

We must not say "your child will definitely see the change" or anything close to it — even though we believe it, and even though we work hard to make it true.

Why: the government's consumer authority has been fining coaching and education companies heavily for promising results — over ₹1.39 crore across 31 institutes, including ₹10 lakh and ₹8 lakh fines in the last few months. We prepare children for KAS, JEE and NEET, so these rules apply to us directly.

And there is a practical reason too: if a family does everything right and still does not see what they hoped for, a promise leaves us with nothing to say. A promise we cannot keep is worse than no promise at all.

Say it the other way round instead — and it becomes our strength

We promise our side. Parents tell the outcome story. "My daughter has changed" from another mother is more believable than "your child will change" from us — and it is safe.

And we can say something almost nobody else in this industry can: "We will not promise you a result. Nobody honestly can — and anyone who does is selling you something."

In a market full of guaranteed-rank advertising, being the one who refuses to promise is a real advantage. Please keep that line in both English and Kannada.

ONE DECISION FOR GOUTAM, NOT GEETA JI

Should we offer to put the ₹999 towards the ₹4,999 if they upgrade within 30 days?

It costs us nothing — we keep the ₹999 either way, and if they move up we gain ₹4,000 we would not have had. It answers "I want to see it work first" completely. And it turns our smallest programme from a step down into a stepping stone.

How we will know which one worked

We will send the same five messages to everyone, but starting with a different one for different people. Some get message 1 first, some get message 3 first, and so on. That way we can tell whether message 3 works because of what it says or just because of when it arrives.

Then we count, for each message: how many people replied, clicked, or booked. Not how many enrolled — with our current numbers that is too few to tell us anything. The replies are the signal.

SO PLEASE — give each message a clear, single thing to do. "Reply YES", "tap here to pick a time", "reply with the one that worries you most". If two messages ask for the same thing, we cannot tell them apart.

8 Writing the messages

WhatsApp does not let a business send whatever it likes. There are three rules that change how we work, and they are worth understanding before you start writing.

Rule one — every message must be approved before we can send it

We write the message, send it to WhatsApp, and wait. Approval usually takes one to two days. So the words must be ready well before a campaign, never on the morning of it.

Rule two — English and Kannada count as two separate messages

We cannot write one message with both languages in it. Each language is submitted and approved separately. So 20 messages actually means 40 to write, approve and keep updated. This is why we are starting with English and Kannada only, and adding other languages later.

Rule three — "helpful" messages cost about 7 times less than "selling" messages

WhatsApp charges us differently depending on what the message does:

KindExampleRoughly
Helpful"Your child's scorecard is ready." · "Here is your login code." · "Your batch starts Monday."15 paise
Selling"Only 3 seats left — book now." · "20% off this week."₹1.09

So for every message, please tell us which kind it is. The test is simple: if you removed the offer, would the person still want this message? If yes, it is helpful. If the whole point is the offer, it is selling — and that is fine, we just need to know, because selling messages are also the ones people block us for.

9 Five things we must never do

These are not preferences. Some are the law, and the computer will be built to refuse them.

1 · We never message a child, and we never use a child's results to sell

Every message goes to the parent. And this part matters: we may tell a parent their child's scorecard is ready — but we may not use how well the child did to decide who gets an offer.

Why: Indian law does not allow companies to track children's behaviour or target advertising at them, and it does not matter if the parent agrees — permission does not make it allowed. So a child's marks, timings and progress can be reported to the parent, but never used to decide our selling.

2 · We never message someone who has not agreed to hear from us

We keep a record of when they agreed and exactly what they were shown. If anyone ever complains, that record is our answer.

3 · Once someone says stop, we stop forever

No exceptions, not even a helpful message. Explained in section 1 — this is the mistake that costs us the most.

4 · We describe messages honestly

If a message is really selling something, we call it selling — even though it costs more. Trying to pass a selling message off as a helpful one risks WhatsApp restricting our whole account, and then nothing gets delivered at all.

5 · English and Kannada only, for now

Adding a language sounds small but doubles the writing, approving and updating work forever. We add one only when there is a real reason.
