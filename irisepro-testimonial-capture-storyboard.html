Stories of transformation — captured without asking for one

Ask a learner to describe how they changed and you get “it was good, I learnt a lot.” Not because nothing happened — because noticing is the hard part, and a fourteen-year-old has no reason to have done it. This is a capture flow that does the noticing for them, an admin surface to run it, and the three shapes the story goes out in — a carousel, a reel, a ninety-second cut.

The one idea everything else follows from. YASHHO already holds the evidence: the scorecard, the AACE deltas, the task history, the Yashodarshi’s actual words. So we never ask “how did you change?” — we show them what changed and ask them to react. Reacting is easy. Remembering is not.

Why these four and not a form. Q1 is answerable only because we showed the number — the question answers itself emotionally, they just say it out loud. Q2 catches the moment while it is still recoverable; asked a month later it is gone. Q3 is the credibility beat, because anyone can peak once. Q4 converts a feeling into a declaration with a date on it, which is the only part that moves the reader to act.
02

Talking beats typing

A thirty-second voice note is easier to give and far more moving to receive. Children will not write a paragraph; they will happily talk. We transcribe, trim, and read it back for approval.

The rule that keeps it honest: we may cut, never add. Every published word was theirs. It is the difference between a testimonial and a advertisement wearing one’s face.
03

What comes back to them

Not a form that vanishes. Their own words, their own numbers, theirs to send to their grandmother — before it goes anywhere else.

c · Publish

One switch per destination, each honouring the consent level the learner picked: private card · storefront · parent comms · Yashodarshi training set. Withdrawing consent pulls it from all of them — the same one-tap the app already promises.

05

Before we build any of it

Run it by hand with ten learners this week. Open their scorecard, find the biggest AACE delta and the sharpest Yashodarshi line, ring them, ask the four questions in order. Fifteen minutes each.

  • You will learn which question actually opens people up — and it will not be all four.
  • You will hear whether the mirror (“41 → 72”) does the work we think it does.
  • You will find out what a child says when asked to declare something, which is the riskiest screen here.

Then build the in-app flow around the questions that worked, not around all of them.

06

The part to get right, not fast

Three of these screens are aimed at minors. Given the posture YASHHO has already taken — payment-corroborated guardian consent, erasure that anonymises in place and keeps the consent ledger — a child’s face and words on a public page is a separate consent from the one given at enrolment, and it should look and feel separate.

  • The ask must never read as a condition of the programme, or of getting the scorecard.
  • Default to first name only. Full name and class is an opt-up, never a default.
  • Withdrawal is one tap, no reason required, and it must actually unpublish.
  • For under-13s the parent voice is not a fallback — it is often the stronger story, because the noticing comes from outside the learner.
A story taken without full consent is worth less than no story — it is the one asset that turns from proof into liability the moment anyone questions how it was obtained.
07

The 90-second cut — and the 200-word budget

A story nobody finishes moves nobody. A teenager speaking conversationally runs 130–150 words a minute, and a testimonial is read slower than prose. So the whole share is about 200 words — four beats, fifty words each. That is not a style note. It is the spec.

0:00 – 0:10The confessionOpen at the lowest point. No setup, no “I joined YASHHO in March.”
0:10 – 0:45One sceneOne task, one person, one thing that was said. Not a summary of ten weeks.
0:45 – 1:05The ordinary TuesdayWhat they do now — and who noticed it without being asked.
1:05 – 1:30The declaration“I’m someone who ___,” with a date on it. Then the number. Then the logo.

What it sounds like at full length

“I used to write really long answers. Not because I knew more — because if I wrote enough, nobody could tell which part I wasn’t sure about.

There was a task about a decision I regretted. I wrote four pages, and my Yashodarshi sent back one line: which one sentence would you keep? I sat with that for two days. I was angry about it, actually.

Then I kept one sentence. It was the only true thing on the page.

Now I write that sentence first. In class, in my notebook, when my mother asks me something. Last month my class teacher asked me to stop putting my hand up so often, so others could go.

I’m someone who says the true thing first. By December I want to be the one they ask to open the debate.”

Read what she never says. Never “confident”, never “grateful”, never the programme’s name. The teacher noticing does work no self-description can — nobody can credibly report their own transformation. And it ends unfinished, on a December nobody has seen yet. That is the only line in it that makes a stranger want to come back.

Six rules, ordered by what they cost when broken

  • Open on a confession, not a claim. “I wrote long answers so nobody could tell” stops a scroll. “YASHHO changed my life” is wallpaper.
  • Someone else must notice. The third-party line is the credibility engine. Without it every other beat reads as a claim.
  • A number, not a superlative. 41 → 72 costs four characters and does what “improved a lot” cannot.
  • One scene, not a summary. Ten weeks compressed into a paragraph is a brochure. One Tuesday is a story.
  • End on an open loop. A declaration with a date leaves the viewer needing to know whether she does it. That is the click — not the CTA.
  • The product appears in the last five seconds. The story sells. The logo only says where to go.

One capture, four lengths — never go back and ask again

0:12 · the card

One sentence + the delta. The atom; everything else expands it.

0:45 · the beat

Confession + scene, or Tuesday + declaration. Three of these per sitting.

1:30 · the arc

All four beats, one face, one cut. The storefront version.

∞ · the archive

Full transcript, unpublished. Where next quarter’s copy comes from.

This is why each capture screen caps an answer at ~30 seconds. Not rationing — it is the edit, done at source. Four thirty-second answers are the 90-second cut, in order, already the right length. An uncapped answer has to be rescued in an edit suite, and the rescuing is where a learner’s words quietly become ours.
08

Same capture → UGC reels and shorts

The four beats were built to be spoken, so the video version needs no second process: add “record instead of type” to the same four screens and the reel is already shot, in order, at the right length.

Do not film it. UGC works because it looks unproduced — a real room, real light, a phone held slightly wrong. The moment there is a crew, a backdrop or a ring light, the viewer reclassifies it as an advertisement and stops believing the number. The learner’s own front camera is not a compromise; it is the format.

What the capture screen must do differently for video

  • Vertical, front camera, one take, 30 seconds. A retake button, not an edit timeline — the second take is always better and the fourth is always worse.
  • Show the prompt over the viewfinder so they answer a question instead of performing to a lens.
  • No script. The instant a child reads, the eyes go dead and everything after it reads as coached.
  • Captions are not optional. Most of this is watched on mute; an uncaptioned reel is a silent film about a person talking.
  • The only graphic is their own number. No app screenshots, no b-roll, no music that tells you how to feel.
  • Under-13s: the parent holds the phone and answers. Not a fallback — often the better film, because the noticing comes from outside the child, and nobody has to watch a small person perform.
Two a week beats twenty in a month. Reels compound on cadence, not polish, and a flow that ships one 15-second declaration every week will out-perform a quarterly batch of beautiful films. Which is the real argument for building this into the product rather than commissioning it: the ask has to sit where the feeling already is.

Consent is a different question for video

A face is not a sentence. The consent screen must name the format explicitly — “a video of you, on Instagram and YouTube” — and a learner who consents to a written quote has not consented to a reel. Same ladder, asked again: private · voice only · face. Withdrawal must pull the published video too, which means the admin has to hold the destination list, not just the file.

The carousel — the one format the system can render by itself

A reel needs a person willing to be on camera. A carousel needs only the four answers we already have, set in type over the delta. Nobody films, nobody edits, nobody waits for a face. It is the same story, rendered — which makes it the first thing to build and the one that will actually ship weekly.

What makes a carousel work that does not apply to a reel

  • Slide 1 is the whole post. It is the only one most people ever see, and it earns every other slide. Put no branding on it — a logo on slide 1 tells the scroll it is an ad before the sentence gets a chance.
  • Every slide must end slightly unfinished. Swipe-through is the only metric that matters; a slide that resolves is a slide people stop on.
  • Six slides, not ten. The four beats plus a pull-quote and a close. More than eight and completion collapses.
  • 4:5, not square. More vertical screen for the same scroll, and the type can be big enough to read without pinching.
  • The shortest slide carries the turn. Whitespace reads as weight. “I sat with that for two days” alone on a slide does more than a paragraph explaining it.
  • The CTA lives in the caption, never on a slide. The slides are her story; the caption is ours.
This is the build order, and it falls out of the format rather than from preference. The carousel is deterministic — transcript in, six slides out, one admin click, no consent for a face and no scheduling anybody. The reel needs a willing learner, a parent who agrees to a camera and a person to cut it. So build the carousel renderer first, ship weekly from day one, and let the reels arrive when a story is good enough to be worth chasing.
