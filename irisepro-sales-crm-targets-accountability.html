A plain guide for everyone in the field and the office

Targets, pace and accountabilitywho does what, day by day

Every person in the field has a number to meet, and every manager has a team to look after. This guide explains where those numbers come from, what each person is expected to do every day and every week to stay close to them, and what happens, honestly and fairly, when someone falls behind. It is written to be read by a YSP on a phone as easily as by the CEO on a laptop.

The basics, in four short answers

Before the routines make sense, here is what a target is and how one becomes real.

What is a target?

A target is a simple set of numbers, kept for a day, a week, a month or a quarter: how many calls, how many visits, and how many sales. A target can be set for the whole network, for one region, for everyone in a role, or for one person's post. When two of these apply at the same time, the more specific one wins for that person. If nobody has set a number at any level, the screen shows only what the person has actually done. It never shows a zero in place of a target, because a zero would read like a failure when the truth is that no one has set a number yet.

Who sets a target?

The General Manager proposes it. The CEO approves it. Only then does it take effect, and from that moment it is a dated version that nobody edits. If the number needs to change later, that is a new proposal and a new approval, with a reason on the record. The GM can never approve his own proposal, a vacant post can neither propose nor approve, and the people who are measured by a target never decide it for themselves. This is deliberate: it keeps the conversation about the number honest, and it means every change has two names against it.

When does a sale count?

A sale counts toward a target on the day the child submits their first task. Until then, money that has been paid is shown clearly as "awaiting start". This way the family's payment is never hidden, and the target is never credited before the work has begun. The same rule is used everywhere: on the YSP's Today screen, in the daily email, in the team board, and in the leaderboards when they open. There is one definition, so no two screens can disagree.

Who sees my number?

You see your own target and your own pace. Your RSM sees the team's pace for the region. The GM sees every region, and the CEO sees the network. Nobody outside that line sees your individual numbers, and nobody is ranked publicly. The board marks the two people who need help today; it does not publish a league table of individuals.

The CEO, the GM and the Head Office desk can also walk down from the network to one region, its RSM and one person, and read the reason, the detail and the action recorded there. The reasons carry our own words, and the short list of reasons comes from configuration, so it can be reworded without a new release. Every one of those views is written into the audit log; there is no quiet look.

A day in the CRM

The day has a rhythm, and it is the same rhythm for everyone. Nothing here asks a person to stare at a dashboard; each moment has one small job.

09:30
The day opensThe working window for the field is 09:30 to 19:30, Monday to Saturday. On Today, each person sees their calls, visits and sales as "done against what should be done by now". When they are ahead, the line stays quiet.

What you see

The routines above are not paperwork. Each one is a small screen, designed for a phone in the field and a laptop in the office. Here they are, in the order a person meets them during a day, and then the screens the leadership uses to see what needs attention, to walk from the network down to one person, and to see what has gone past its time.

What each person does

The same numbers, seen from four chairs. Each person has a short routine and a clear limit on what they are asked to do.

The YSP

Meet the day's number

Ravi works his own families. His job is to do the calls, the visits and the sales, and to say plainly when something outside his control got in the way.

Every morning

He opens Today and looks at one line: how he stands against the number for this hour. If he is ahead, the line is quiet and he simply works. If he is behind, the screen tells him what closes the gap.

During the day

He works his follow-ups and planned visits. At most two nudges arrive, late morning and mid-afternoon, and only if he is genuinely behind. He is never asked to keep the phone open or to check in at set times.

At the end of the day

If he is behind, he is offered three taps: what got in the way, what would help, and a note if he wants one. He can skip it. If he has a sale that has been paid but not started, it sits beside his sales line as "awaiting start" so it is never forgotten.

What he is not asked to do

  • Share his location. The CRM never reads it.
  • Explain himself more than once. One check-in is enough.
  • Compete publicly. He sees his own pace and his team's top three, not a ranking of everyone.
The RSM

Help the team, not police it

Suresh runs a region. His job is to know by mid-morning who needs help and to bring the right kind of help by the afternoon.

Every morning

His agenda names the people who were behind three days running, with the reason each one gave, the line they wrote about what happened, and the ones who have gone quiet. He starts his day with those names, not with a spreadsheet.

Mid-morning and mid-afternoon

The team pace board shows every YSP in his region as one row: calls, visits, sales, and when they last did something. The two people furthest behind are marked. The mark is an invitation to a conversation, not a punishment, and no row ever shows a location.

Anything past its time

The clock list puts the region's late things in one place, oldest first: families past their stage limit, the unowned queue, an approval waiting, a miss line with no action. Each carries its owner and how it was delivered. He acknowledges with a note; an alert nobody acknowledges steps up to the GM, and then to the CEO. Acknowledged is not cleared — the row stays until the thing itself moves.

At four o'clock

He takes the names and the reasons into his afternoon round and the update he sends. Where he can help, he does: a joint visit, a different plan, a coaching session. He records the action he chose.

Every week

When his region misses its weekly number, he records one line for the region: the main reason and the action he is taking. That line goes up to the GM with the region's numbers. If the region misses two weeks running, the GM may open a target review.

What he is not asked to do

  • Chase people with hourly messages. The system sends at most two, and only to the person concerned.
  • Decide the target. He can ask the GM for a change, with a reason, but he cannot set it.
  • Watch anyone's location. The board shows activity, never coordinates.
The General Manager

Own the numbers, and the reasons

The GM is the one person who proposes targets and the only person who can open a target review. He reads the whole state, but he works through the people who run it.

Every Monday

He opens the accountability card. It lists the regions that missed last week, the reason each one recorded, the action the RSM chose, and any YSP who was behind three days. He reads it with the week's other numbers, not instead of them.

Through the week

He watches the network from one screen: every region and every post, each number in force, each level resolved on its own. When something needs to change, he proposes it: old number to new number, for which level, from which date, and why. The CEO decides.

When a number worries him

He opens the same drill-down the CEO and the HO desk use. The attention band names the regions behind today, the reviews open and the proposals waiting. He can walk to a region, its RSM and one YSP, and read the reasons and actions there. The pipeline view beneath it says whether the families are moving and where they are piling up: families in each stage, moves in and out this week, weeks to clear and the families that have gone quiet.

What is late, and what is slowing

The clock list shows the network's late stages, approvals and proposals with the level that owns each. He acknowledges what is his, sees the escalations that nobody below picked up, and reads the speed line: moves this week against last, and the places that are slowing or stuck. His own desk has a stated time too, and it is on the same list.

At month end

When a region has missed two weeks running, he may open a target review, which is itself a proposal the CEO decides. He also records the network's monthly line: the main reason, and the action being taken. If the network misses a month, that line goes to the CEO's month close.

What he is not asked to do

  • Edit a target directly. Every change is a proposed version, approved by the CEO and written by the system.
  • Approve his own proposal. The engine compares the person, not just the post.
  • Read a reason without an action. Every miss he sees carries what was done about it.
The CEO

Decide, and set the standard

The CEO is the only person who can make a target real, and the only one who sees the network when it misses a month.

Before the morning call

One screen tells him what needs attention: the regions behind pace today, the reviews open, the proposals waiting on him, the approvals that have waited more than a day and the miss lines with no action recorded. Each one opens the thing that explains it. Beneath it the clock list names everything past its stated time with the level that owns it, and the escalations nobody below picked up arrive with him. From there he walks the tree — network, region, the region's RSM and one person — and reads the numbers, the reason, the line the person wrote and the action taken. He can also see where the pipeline is piling up, stage by stage, and whether it is moving at all. He changes nothing from that screen; it is there to point him at the right conversation.

When a proposal waits

Everything only the CEO can decide sits in one queue: target versions now, prices and access later. Each row shows who proposed it, what changes, why, and how long it has waited. He approves, or rejects with a note. A rejection changes nothing and returns to the proposer with the note attached.

At month and quarter end

He receives the network's line, the reasons counted by level, and the actions already taken. Where the same reason appears across regions, that is a conversation about the plan, not about the people. He can ask the GM to bring a review, and he decides it.

What he is not asked to do

  • Approve his own proposal, or one whose next step belongs to another role.
  • Read a person's card in secret. He reaches it only through the drill-down, the detail stays inside the management line, and every view is written into the audit log.
  • Chase the week. The Monday card and the month close keep the rhythm; the queue is there when a decision is actually needed.
And the office behind it

A proposal becomes a real target in one way only. The GM proposes; the CEO approves; the system writes a new dated version. Nobody types over an old number, and the record keeps both names and the reason. If anything were ever applied without that record, the system would refuse it. This is what makes the numbers worth trusting when someone is held to them.

When a number is missed

Missing a day is normal. What matters is that somebody knows why, sooner rather than later, and that something sensible happens next. Here is the ladder, in the order it happens.

One day behind

Nothing happens beyond the line on Today and, if the gap is large enough, one of the two daily nudges. There is no meeting, no message to the manager, and no mark against anyone's name.

Close of day, and the person says why

If they choose to, the YSP taps a short reason from the list (no leads left, the market was off, leave, data entered late, support needed, or something else), writes one or two lines about what actually happened, and picks an action if one is obvious. The list of reasons and actions is configuration, so it can be added to or reworded without a new release. It is a check-in, not a confession, and it is always optional.

Three days running

The person appears on their RSM's morning agenda with the reason they gave, the line they wrote and the action chosen. The RSM comes to them with help, which usually means a joint visit, a different plan or some coaching, and records what was chosen.

A region behind at week end

The RSM records one line for the region and chooses an action. That line, with the region's numbers, sits on the GM's Monday card. The GM is reading a region's reason, not naming a person.

Two weeks running

The GM may open a target review. This is a formal conversation about whether the plan or the number is wrong. Opening it is the GM's decision alone, and deciding it is the CEO's.

A month behind, across the network

The network's monthly line reaches the CEO with the reasons counted by level and the actions already taken. If the same reason repeats across regions, the answer is a change of plan, not a round of blame.

The one rule that matters in this ladder

What travels up the ladder is the action, not the excuse. A reason explains a day; an action changes the next one. That is why every line, at every level, carries both, and why the person who missed the number still owns the answer to it.

What keeps it fair

Accountability only works if people trust how it is done. These are the promises that make it something people can work with.

This board never uses location.

The pace board reads activity counts and the time of the last activity — not where anyone is. Location is recorded separately and narrowly: a fix at the start and close of a visit, and a coarse heartbeat (about 110 m) while the field app is open in working hours. Only the RSM, GM and HO can see coordinates; a YSP sees only whether his own location is on. It is never a trail and never a street address.

Two messages a day at most, for the person in the field.

No hourly pings to the YSP, no evening messages, nothing on Sunday. If someone is ahead, they are left alone. The clock alerts are a different thing: they go to the manager who owns the late work, never to the person doing it, and they carry counts and post codes, not names.

One rule for a sale.

A sale is confirmed when the child submits the first task, everywhere. Money paid but not started stays visible as "awaiting start".

No silent zeros.

Where no target has been set, the screen shows the count alone. Nobody is shown as failing a number that was never given to them.

A check-in, not a block.

The end-of-day reason is optional. It cannot stop a person from working, and it is never used on its own to judge them.

Two names on every change.

The GM proposes; the CEO approves. A person never sets the number they are measured by, and a vacant post decides nothing.

The line the person wrote stays in the line of command.

Their RSM reads the detail, and so do the levels above once the miss climbs — the name and the words travel only that far. It never goes into an email, a push, a report or a league table, and every look is logged.

The reasons are ours to change.

The short lists of reasons and actions live in configuration, in English and Kannada. A new reason is added by a governed change, not by waiting for a release, and a stored reason never disappears when the list is reworded.

Help comes with the mark.

A person is marked on the board so the RSM can help them, not so the team can watch them fail. The bottom two are marked; nobody is ranked.

Nothing is left to go quiet.

Every stage, queue, approval, proposal and miss line has a stated limit and an owner. Past that time it appears on that owner's list, is delivered at least daily — hourly for the short clocks — and steps up a level if nobody acknowledges it. Acknowledging is not clearing: the row stays until the thing itself moves, and both times are kept. If the monitor fails, engineering is paged.
